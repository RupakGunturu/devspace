An **MVP (Minimum Viable Product)** is the smallest version of your product that lets you test your core hypothesis with real users — nothing more, nothing less.

> The goal isn't to build a perfect product. The goal is to learn what you should have built in the first place.

---

## What an MVP Is (and Isn't)

| Is | Isn't |
|---|---|
| The smallest thing that tests your riskiest assumption | A half-finished product |
| A learning tool, not a revenue tool | An excuse for poor quality |
| Something you can ship in weeks, not months | A prototype that never sees users |
| Deliberately limited scope | An accidental MVP (just not having time to finish) |

<details>
<summary>Types of MVPs you can build today</summary>

### 1. Landing Page MVP
Build a single page describing your product. Run ads. Measure how many people sign up or click "request access." If nobody cares, pivot before writing a line of code.

**Example:** Buffer started as a landing page with a "plans and pricing" button. When people clicked, they saw a message: "We're not ready yet — want to be notified?" That validated demand before the product existed.

### 2. Wizard of Oz MVP
The frontend looks automated, but behind the scenes a human is doing the work. Users think they're using a product; they're actually interacting with a person.

**Example:** Zappos founder personally bought and shipped shoes from retail stores to test if people would buy shoes online. No warehouse, no inventory — just manual fulfillment.

### 3. Concierge MVP
Instead of building software, manually deliver the value your product promises to a small group of users.

**Example:** A food delivery startup could start by taking orders over WhatsApp and delivering personally, before building an app.

### 4. Single-Feature MVP
Build one feature — the one that solves the core pain — and nothing else. Resist the urge to add "just one more thing."

**Example:** Dropbox's MVP was a 3-minute demo video. No product existed. The waiting list went from 5,000 to 75,000 overnight.

</details>

## The MVP Framework

| Step | Action | Question to Answer |
|---|---|---|
| 1. **Identify your riskiest assumption** | What must be true for this product to work? | "Will people actually pay for this?" |
| 2. **Design the smallest experiment** | What's the cheapest way to test that assumption? | "Can I test this without building software?" |
| 3. **Set a success threshold** | What result would make you pivot vs. persevere? | "How many signups = enough signal?" |
| 4. **Ship and measure** | Get it in front of real users fast | "Are people using it? Coming back? Paying?" |
| 5. **Learn and iterate** | Use the data to decide next steps | "Did the assumption hold? What did I miss?" |

---

## The MVP Anti-Patterns

- **The forever MVP:** "It's still an MVP" after 12 months. At some point, ship a real product.
- **The stealth MVP:** Building in secret for months. If nobody sees it, you can't learn from it.
- **The feature creep MVP:** "Minimum" means minimum. If your MVP has 15 features, you built too much.
- **The no-feedback MVP:** Shipping an MVP without a way to collect user feedback is just building in the dark.
